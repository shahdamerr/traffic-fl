\chapter{Background and Literature Review}
\label{ch:background}

This chapter reviews the theoretical and methodological foundations of traffic flow prediction (TFP) and Federated Learning (FL) in Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITS). It first presents the evolution of traffic forecasting methods from traditional statistical models to deep learning and graph-based approaches. It then discusses the emergence of FL as a decentralized learning paradigm for ITS, with particular focus on two challenges central to this thesis: statistical heterogeneity caused by non-IID traffic distributions and communication overhead in bandwidth-constrained edge environments. Finally, the chapter identifies the research gap addressed by the proposed lightweight hierarchical federated learning framework.

\section{Traditional and Deep Learning Approaches to Traffic Flow Prediction}

Traffic flow prediction has long been a core task in intelligent transportation planning. In this thesis, traffic flow prediction refers specifically to short-term traffic speed forecasting from loop-detector sensor observations. Early approaches relied on centralized statistical and machine learning models, including ARIMA \cite{arima1970}, Vector Auto-Regression (VAR) \cite{var2005}, Support Vector Regression (SVR) combined with dimensionality reduction techniques such as PCA \cite{jin2008}, and fuzzy C-means neural networks \cite{tang2017}. Although these methods provided the foundation for data-driven traffic forecasting, they were limited in their ability to model the nonlinear and dynamic spatio-temporal dependencies present in large-scale traffic networks.

With the growth of deep learning, traffic forecasting shifted toward neural architectures capable of learning complex temporal and spatio-temporal patterns. Recurrent models such as Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks \cite{hochreiter1997} and Gated Recurrent Units (GRUs) \cite{cho2014} became widely used for modeling sequential traffic observations. In particular, GRU-based Encoder-Decoder (Seq2Seq) architectures have proven effective for multi-step forecasting because they encode historical traffic observations into a latent representation and decode future predictions autoregressively across the forecast horizon \cite{zhu2025cmba}.

To explicitly model spatial dependencies among road sensors, researchers introduced Spatio-Temporal Graph Neural Networks (STGNNs). Li et al. \cite{li2018} proposed the Diffusion Convolutional Recurrent Neural Network (DCRNN), which models traffic speed forecasting as a diffusion process over a directed graph and popularized the METR-LA benchmark dataset used in this thesis. Wu et al. \cite{wu2019graphwavenet} introduced Graph WaveNet, combining adaptive adjacency learning with dilated temporal convolutions. Guo et al. \cite{guo2019} proposed ASTGCN, which applies attention mechanisms to capture dynamic spatial and temporal correlations. More recent graph-based models, such as AGCN \cite{qi2023fedagcn}, further account for asynchronous spatial-temporal dependencies, where the influence of neighboring nodes propagates with time delays.

Although STGNN-based methods achieve strong centralized forecasting performance, they typically assume that all sensor observations and graph structures are available at a central server. This assumption becomes increasingly difficult to maintain in large-scale ITS deployments, where continuous data transmission creates communication overhead and limits the use of distributed edge resources. These limitations motivate the use of Federated Learning for decentralized traffic forecasting.

\section{Federated Learning in Intelligent Transportation Systems}

Federated Learning (FL), introduced by McMahan et al. \cite{mcmahan2017}, enables multiple distributed clients to collaboratively train a shared model without directly transmitting raw data. Instead, each client performs local training on its private dataset and communicates only model parameters or gradients to an aggregation server. This makes FL particularly relevant to ITS, where traffic sensors and edge devices continuously generate large volumes of location-specific data.

Surveys by Kairouz et al. \cite{kairouz2021} and Chong et al. \cite{chong2025} identify FL as a promising paradigm for distributed ITS applications, but also highlight persistent challenges. Two of these challenges are central to this thesis. First, traffic sensor data are highly heterogeneous: different road segments exhibit different speed distributions, congestion patterns, and temporal dynamics. This violates the IID assumption commonly made by standard FL algorithms. Second, repeated transmission of model updates can create substantial communication overhead, especially when deep learning models are trained over many communication rounds.

The following sections review existing work addressing these two challenges through non-IID mitigation, clustering-based personalization, communication-efficient aggregation, and FL-based traffic forecasting.

\subsection{Data Heterogeneity and Non-IID Traffic Distributions}

A major challenge in FL is statistical heterogeneity, where client datasets are non-Independent and Identically Distributed (non-IID). In traffic prediction, this problem is especially pronounced. Highway sensors often observe high-speed, low-variance traffic patterns, while urban or arterial sensors may exhibit stop-and-go dynamics, congestion peaks, and stronger temporal variability. When standard FedAvg is applied to such heterogeneous clients, local updates may move the global model in conflicting directions, a phenomenon commonly referred to as \textit{client drift} \cite{zhao2018, karimireddy2020}.

General-purpose FL methods address non-IID data using several strategies. FedProx \cite{li2020fedprox} regularizes local training by penalizing excessive deviation from the global model. SCAFFOLD \cite{karimireddy2020} corrects client drift using control variates, while MOON \cite{li2021moon} aligns local and global representations through model-contrastive learning. More recent work has explored adaptive regularization mechanisms that adjust to the severity of client distribution shift \cite{cha2025}. These approaches improve stability under heterogeneous data, but they are not specifically designed around the spatial and temporal structure of traffic sensor networks.

In the ITS domain, several methods explicitly address traffic heterogeneity. Dai et al. \cite{dai2024} proposed a reputation-based personalized FL framework in which aggregation weights depend on client training quality. FedGDAN \cite{li2025fedgdan} introduced adaptive local aggregation to handle non-IID traffic distributions across road segments. FedGL \cite{zhang2024fedgl} and pFedCTP \cite{zhang2024pfedctp} further investigate structural and cross-city heterogeneity by selectively sharing transferable traffic patterns while preserving location-specific information.

These studies show that aggregation based only on global model averaging or sample count is insufficient for traffic forecasting when client distributions differ substantially. Effective FL systems for ITS must account for differences in local traffic behavior, either through personalization, adaptive aggregation, or clustering.

\subsection{Clustering-Based Approaches to Data Heterogeneity}
\label{sec:clustering}

Rather than attempting to force all clients into a single global model, clustering-based FL embraces heterogeneity by grouping clients with similar data distributions. Each cluster can then train a specialized model, reducing intra-cluster variability while avoiding the loss of local traffic characteristics. This approach is particularly suitable for traffic prediction, where sensors naturally form behavioral groups such as steady-flow highways, congested ramps, and arterial roads.

Feng et al. \cite{feng2024} proposed FedTFP, a two-stage federated traffic flow prediction framework that partitions road networks using Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) and K-Means clustering. Their results showed that traffic-pattern-based partitioning outperformed random and topology-based strategies, demonstrating that temporal similarity can be a more effective basis for federated grouping than simple geographic or graph-based partitioning.

Related work has also explored clustering and sub-network partitioning in traffic forecasting. FedAGCN \cite{qi2023fedagcn} partitions road networks into sub-graphs and selectively aggregates model components to reduce spatial conflicts. FLAGCN \cite{yaqub2025flagcn} similarly combines federated learning with graph partitioning to balance forecasting accuracy and parameter efficiency.

Hierarchical FL methods extend this idea by introducing multiple levels of aggregation. MFVSTGNN \cite{liu2023mfvstgnn} integrates a Variational Graph Autoencoder with a spatio-temporal graph neural network in a multilevel FL architecture, showing that hierarchical structures can improve forecasting beyond flat single-server aggregation.

Overall, clustering-based methods demonstrate that grouping clients by traffic behavior can mitigate non-IID divergence. However, many existing clustering approaches either train clusters in isolation or rely on graph-heavy architectures. This motivates a lightweight hierarchical approach that preserves cluster specialization while still allowing controlled knowledge sharing between clusters.

\subsection{Communication-Efficient Federated Aggregation}

Communication overhead is another major barrier to FL deployment in ITS. Traffic forecasting models must be trained over many communication rounds, and transmitting full model updates from all clients can quickly become expensive in bandwidth-constrained edge networks. Existing communication-efficient FL methods can be broadly grouped into three categories: gradient compression, client selection, and hierarchical aggregation.

Gradient compression reduces bandwidth by transmitting only a subset or compressed representation of model updates. For example, FedCE \cite{gao2024fedce} applies Top-K gradient sparsification and adaptive aggregation to reduce uplink communication while maintaining competitive forecasting accuracy. Although compression can significantly reduce bandwidth, aggressive sparsification may harm convergence when model updates are noisy or highly heterogeneous, especially during early training.

Client selection reduces communication by allowing only a subset of clients to participate in each round. Oort \cite{lai2021oort} selects clients based on data utility and system efficiency, while mobility-aware strategies such as MACS \cite{albelaihi2025} consider connectivity and resource availability in IoT environments.

Hierarchical and decentralized aggregation reduce communication bottlenecks by distributing aggregation across multiple levels or neighboring clients. AS-AFL \cite{ma2025asafl} combines intra-group peer-to-peer aggregation with inter-group asynchronous learning, while CEDFL \cite{wang2024cedfl} adaptively controls neighbor communication probabilities to reduce bandwidth. FedTFormer \cite{shuai2025fedtformer} applies hierarchical FL with edge servers and cluster-specific fine-tuning for long-term traffic prediction.

In traffic forecasting specifically, CMBA-FL \cite{zhu2025cmba} combines a GRU-based Encoder-Decoder model with a parameter relevance detector that filters out irrelevant model parameters before upload, achieving a strong accuracy--communication tradeoff on the METR-LA dataset. This work is especially relevant because it demonstrates that lightweight recurrent architectures can remain competitive in FL-based traffic prediction while offering a more communication-efficient alternative to heavier graph or Transformer-based models.

\subsection{Federated Learning for Traffic Flow Prediction}

Several studies have directly applied FL to traffic flow prediction. FedGRU \cite{liu2020fedgru} was among the earlier FL-based traffic prediction frameworks, using recurrent models to preserve data locality while forecasting traffic conditions. CNFGNN \cite{meng2021cnfgnn} introduced a cross-node federated graph neural network that enables spatial information sharing without centralizing raw data.

Recent graph-based FL methods have improved the ability to recover spatial dependencies under decentralized settings. FedGTP \cite{yang2024fedgtp} uses adaptive polynomial mechanisms to model inter-client spatial dependencies under privacy constraints. FedGM \cite{wang2024fedgm} combines graph federated learning with meta-learning to support adaptation to unseen traffic network structures. FedHINT \cite{zhou2026fedhint} addresses the loss of global spatial information by constructing proxy nodes that approximate cross-regional dependencies while still avoiding raw feature sharing.

These methods demonstrate the growing importance of FL for traffic forecasting. However, many of the strongest approaches rely on graph reconstruction, proxy-node generation, or complex spatio-temporal backbones. While effective, such designs can increase architectural complexity and communication cost, making them less suitable for lightweight edge-based deployments.

\section{Research Gap and Thesis Positioning}
\label{sec:gap}

The reviewed literature shows that FL-based traffic forecasting has advanced along three main directions: improving personalization under non-IID data, reducing communication overhead, and recovering spatial dependencies without centralizing raw sensor observations. However, these directions are often addressed separately rather than within a unified lightweight framework.

Global FL methods such as FedAvg struggle with client drift under heterogeneous traffic distributions. Clustering-based methods improve personalization by grouping similar clients, but many train clusters independently and therefore limit beneficial cross-cluster knowledge transfer. Hierarchical FL methods improve scalability, but often rely on graph neural networks or Transformer-based architectures that increase model complexity. Similarly, graph-based federated methods can recover spatial dependencies, but may require adjacency matrices, proxy-node mechanisms, or additional structural information that complicates deployment.

These limitations create a need for a framework that jointly supports:

\begin{itemize}
    \item temporal-pattern-aware client grouping,
    \item cluster-specific model specialization,
    \item controlled inter-cluster knowledge sharing,
    \item lightweight forecasting under resource constraints,
    \item and explicit communication accounting.
\end{itemize}

Building on the GRU-based Encoder-Decoder architecture validated by CMBA-FL \cite{zhu2025cmba}, this thesis proposes a lightweight hierarchical FL framework for traffic flow prediction. The framework addresses the identified gap through two complementary mechanisms:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Intra-cluster specialization:} Sensors are grouped by temporal pattern similarity using DTW-based clustering \cite{feng2024}, and each cluster trains a specialized model using quality-weighted federated aggregation \cite{dai2024}.
    
    \item \textbf{Inter-cluster generalization:} Cluster models periodically exchange knowledge through a DTW-distance-weighted hierarchical aggregation step, allowing similar traffic-pattern groups to share information without collapsing all clusters into a single global model.
\end{enumerate}

Combined with adaptive client selection, the proposed framework directly addresses the two central challenges identified in this chapter: non-IID traffic heterogeneity and communication overhead. Unlike heavy graph- or Transformer-based FL architectures, the proposed approach uses a lightweight GRU Seq2Seq backbone with explicit communication tracking. Unlike isolated clustering methods, it preserves cluster specialization while enabling controlled inter-cluster transfer. This positions the proposed framework as a scalable and communication-aware solution for federated traffic forecasting in ITS.
